Post-Flight Engineering Analysis

📘 Saturn V Systems Engineering Field Guide

1. Thrust-to-Weight Ratio (TWR) & Tsiolkovsky Δv

To leave Pad 39A, your engines must produce more thrust than gravity pulls down (TWR > 1.20 g). Once airborne, your Delta-V (Δv)—calculated via Δv = Isp · g₀ · ln(m_wet / m_dry)—determines how far you can go. Adding heavy payloads increases dry mass, shrinking Δv unless you add higher-efficiency stages (like J-2 vacuum engines) or larger fuel tanks.

2. Power Generation vs. Orbital Eclipse

Scientific instruments and high-gain radios continuously consume electrical power (Watts). Solar panels generate power in sunlight, but every orbit passes through planetary shadow (eclipse). You must carry Battery Packs sized to sustain your power draw through the eclipse window.

3. Communications Downlink & Distance

High-resolution science equipment generates massive data streams (Mbps). Further destinations (GTO, Lunar Orbit) suffer free-space signal attenuation, reducing effective downlink bandwidth. Pair heavy science suites with a Deep-Space High-Gain Dish so data isn't lost.

4. Crew Safety & Atmospheric Re-Entry

Crewed command modules (Apollo CM) and sample-return missions require Parachute Recovery Systems to survive atmospheric re-entry and splashdown. Uncrewed orbital satellites do not need parachutes, saving mass and budget.
